    Preparing your cup

    1. Make a proper Greek coffee in a briki and drink it slowly, leaving the thick grounds at the bottom.
    2. Hold the cup by the handle, make a wish, and swirl it three times so the grounds coat the walls.
    3. Cover it with the saucer and turn it over in one steady motion. Leave it five to ten minutes to cool and dry.
    4. Lift the cup and photograph straight down into it. Photograph the saucer too, if you like.

    How it started: Thea Maria

    This little website began with my Thea Maria, my aunt, the reader of our family's cups.

    When the coffee was finished, the cups were turned over onto their saucers and left to cool, and then, one by one, they found their way to her. She would lift each cup, turn it slowly in her hands, and peer inside as if reading a letter. Then she would tell us our fortunes.

    A bird meant news was coming. A ring meant somebody was in love. A road meant a journey, and a dark patch was a worry about to lift. The way she told it had all of us giggling, teasing each other about whatever she had found, and waiting for our turn.

    But under the laughter there was something more. Her readings left us feeling inspired, a little braver about the things we were hoping for, and a lot of real advice was passed across that table in those moments. Some of it we still repeat to each other today.

    Yiayia here is a small way of keeping that tradition at the table, so anyone can turn their cup over, laugh a little, and leave with something good to think about.

    What reading the cup means

    In Greek it is called καφεμαντεία, kafemanteía, from kafés, coffee, and manteía, divination. The wider world knows it as tasseography. It is only possible because of the way Greek coffee is made: finely ground coffee boiled slowly in a small pot called a bríki, poured unfiltered with its foam, the kaïmáki, into a small cup and served with a glass of cold water. Whether you take it skétos (no sugar), métrios (medium) or glykós (sweet), the thick grounds left at the bottom are what the reader needs.

    Cup reading arrived with coffee itself, travelling through the Ottoman world from the sixteenth century onward, and it is shared today across Greece, Cyprus, Turkey, the Balkans, Armenia and the Middle East. Each place has its own habits and its own favourite symbols, but the heart of it is the same.

    In Greek homes it has mostly lived in the kitchen and the living room rather than the café. A visit, a coffee, and then the cups are turned over while the conversation carries on. The readers were usually the women of the family and the neighbourhood: a yiayia, a thea, a neighbour with a gift for it. Many families hold that you should never read your own cup; someone else has to do it for you.

    Very few people take it as true prophecy, and the Orthodox Church has never approved of fortune-telling. That has never stopped it. A reading is really a gentle conversation: a way to talk about love, work, travel, worries and hopes, with the symbols opening the door. A good reader sees the person as much as the grounds, and the best readings end with laughter and a bit of wisdom you didn't know you needed.

    How the cup is read

    Where a shape sits matters as much as the shape itself. In the Greek way, the bottom of the cup holds the past, the walls the present, and the rim the future.

    The handle stands for you, your heart and your home. Shapes on the far side speak of work, money, travel and other people. Big, clear shapes are strong signs; small, faint ones are gentle hints. A light cup is a light heart; a dark, heavy cup means there is a lot on your mind.

    Finally, the saucer is read for the home. A clean saucer means a peaceful house.
